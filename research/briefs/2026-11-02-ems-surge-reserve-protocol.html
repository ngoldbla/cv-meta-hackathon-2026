EMS Surge Reserve Protocol

This protocol gives three surge levels and the dispatch rules for each level. It is for clinical and dispatch staff to review. It is not approved for use.

Status of this protocol

We tested these rules only in a simulator of NYC 911 calls from 2017 to 2019. No real patient has received this protocol. Clinical staff must review the hold rule before any test. A first test must be advice only: the system suggests the action, and the dispatcher decides.

Priority tiers

The protocol uses three priority tiers. Each tier has a response target. A call is late when the first unit arrives after its target.

Tier 1 · life-threatening15 min targetExample: cardiac arrest. Never held.
Tier 2 · urgent40 min targetCan be held for up to 5 minutes at level 2.
Tier 3 · low acuity120 min targetCan be held for up to 5 minutes at level 2, or sent to the nurse line at level 1.

How to know the surge level

The dispatcher reads two numbers. Both are already on a dispatch board:

  • Calls waiting in each borough: the number of calls in that borough that have no unit.
  • Free units in the city: the number of on-duty units that are idle now, in all boroughs.

Do the check again at each new call and each time a unit becomes free. The levels add together: level 2 also uses all level 1 actions.

Level 0

Normal

Trigger Fewer than 2 calls wait in each borough.
Actions
  • Send the nearest free unit to each call. Take calls in tier order, then the call that has waited longest.
  • Each hour, move idle units to cover the areas with the most expected calls.
Level 1

Borough surge

Trigger 2 or more calls wait in one borough. Existing standard rules (b1)
Actions, in that borough only
  • Request 3 extra units. Do this not more than one time each hour for each borough, and only while the surge budget has hours left (10% extra unit-hours each day).
  • Send new tier-3 calls to the nurse triage line, until the nurse line takes 43% of the tier-3 calls it can take.
Level 2

Citywide reserve

Trigger Fewer than 5 free units in the whole city. New rule found by the study
Actions
  • Tier 3: if the call has waited less than 5 minutes, hold it for 5 minutes. Do not send a unit yet.
  • Tier 2: if there are fewer than 4 free units and the call has waited less than 5 minutes, hold it for 5 minutes.
  • Tier 1: never hold. Send the nearest free unit immediately.
  • After the hold, send the nearest free unit. A call is held one time only.

In the simulator, about 200 units are on duty in NYC at busy times. Thus, "fewer than 5 free" means that about 98% of the fleet is busy. For a different fleet size, use the same share, about 2% of on-duty units, and test it again.

Why the hold helps

When almost every unit is busy, the next free unit is precious. If it goes to a low-acuity call, a cardiac arrest that comes 2 minutes later has no unit. A short hold keeps the last free units for urgent calls. During the hold, other units can become free near the held call. We think that this is why the held tiers also improved, but we did not measure it.

In the simulator, calls that arrive with no free unit fell from 13% to 0.03%. All three tiers were less late, which includes the held tiers.

On calm days, the level 2 trigger seldom occurs, so the rule has almost no effect. The 3% increase on calm days at equal weights is in the range of random variation, but clinical staff must examine it.

The stronger version that we do not recommend yet

A second loop found a version with a larger gain: 26% less lateness on all days and up to 33% less on busy days. It adds three changes. It holds only when 16 or more calls wait in the city. It holds for up to 10 minutes. It requests 2 extra units at a time, not 3. It also uses a learned model to select the unit.

This version was 6–12% worse than the standard rules on the calmest days. The learned model cannot be written as a rule. Thus, we recommend the simpler level 2 rule above.

Items for clinical review

Patient risk

  • A held tier-2 or tier-3 call can be under-triaged. Should the call-taker ask again during the hold?
  • Should some tier-2 call types never be held, for example chest pain or breathing problems?
  • Is 5 minutes an acceptable maximum hold for each tier?
  • Does the hold need a call-back to the caller?

Monitoring and stop rules

  • Record each hold: time, tier, call type and the delay that it caused.
  • Each day, compare tier-1 response times with and without level 2.
  • Stop the test if any held patient has a bad outcome that the hold caused.
  • The dispatcher can always cancel a hold.

Limits

  • The results come from a simulator, not from real dispatch. The simulator matched 7 of 10 real-world checks.
  • The score counts minutes late. It does not count patient outcomes.
  • The sealed tests on real surge periods (Omicron and COVID wave 1) did not run. They must run before a live test.
  • The thresholds come from NYC. A different city must test its own thresholds.
